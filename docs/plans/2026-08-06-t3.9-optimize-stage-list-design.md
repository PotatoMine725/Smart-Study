## 2. Candidate stage lists

All three candidates are ordered lists of `IReadOnlyList<ScheduledItem> → IReadOnlyList<ScheduledItem>`
functions. None reads `SoeWeights` — per D-G/D-J, only the checkpoint mechanism outside the stages is
weight-aware; a stage that read the weight vector to decide its own move would be smuggling objective
knowledge into the "deterministic pipeline" half of D-E, which is exactly the shape D-E rejects.

### Candidate 1 — `N = 1`: `LoadRebalanceStage` only (**recommended**, see §4)

**The move.** Rank days by `(TotalMinutes desc, Date asc)`; take the busiest used day `D_max`, with
total `maxTotal`. Rank the *other* used days by `(TotalMinutes asc, Date asc)` to find the least-loaded
day `D_min`, with total `minTotal` — **`D_min` is always the destination, unconditionally; there is no
deadline-preferring destination step in this stage** (see the correction note below — an earlier draft
had one, and it broke the proof this stage exists to provide). On `D_max`, rank items by
`(SoPhut desc, MaTask asc)` and take the **first item `X` whose `X.SoPhut ≤ maxTotal − minTotal`** (an
eligibility filter against the fixed, precomputed `D_min`, not a search — see "non-combinatorial"
below); if no item on `D_max` is eligible, the stage is a no-op for this pass. Move `X` to `D_min` in
its entirety (no split — see §3 D2). If there is only one used day, the stage is a no-op (its
checkpoint ties the entry state, which G2-2's comparator already handles for free as
`Rejected_NoImprovement`/tie-break-to-lower-`k`).

**Correction — an earlier draft of this section let the destination prefer `Date ≤ X.HanChot` (mirroring
`FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s deadline-safety habit) and it was wrong, caught in a second review pass
after the eligibility filter above was already added.** With a deadline-preferring destination, the
threshold `X.SoPhut ≤ maxTotal − minTotal` is checked against the *global* `minTotal`, but the actual
destination could be a different, more-loaded day `D_dest` (the least-loaded day satisfying the
deadline filter) with `destTotal` anywhere in `[minTotal, maxTotal]`. Concrete counter-example:
`maxTotal = 300`, `minTotal = 60` (that day excluded by the deadline filter for this `X`),
`destTotal = 280`, `X.SoPhut = 200`. `200 ≤ 300 − 60 = 240`, so `X` is "eligible" by the stated rule —
but moving it to `D_dest` yields `280 + 200 = 480`, and the new spread (`480 − 60 = 420`) is *wider*
than the old one (`300 − 60 = 240`). The eligibility filter's proof only holds when the threshold is
checked against the day the move actually lands on. Fix: drop the deadline preference from this stage's
destination choice entirely — always `D_min`, unconditionally, as specified above. This costs nothing
under G2-2 (§3 D3 already notes the deadline preference is "not required... the admissibility gate is
sufficient protection regardless" — true here too, and now load-bearing rather than a nicety).
`FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s own deadline-safety property is unaffected — it comes from *which day
absorbs which chunk* (always the earlier day), not from a destination-ranking step, so it has no
analogous bug.

**Why the eligibility filter (`X.SoPhut ≤ maxTotal − minTotal`), and not just "largest item."** An
earlier draft of this stage moved the unconditionally-largest item on `D_max`, on the reasoning that
the biggest move makes the biggest dent. That is wrong on its own target term: if `X.SoPhut` exceeds
the current gap between the busiest and least-loaded day, moving it *overshoots* — `D_min` becomes the
new busiest day, `D_max` becomes emptier than `D_min` was, and the spread can end up **wider** than
before the move, not narrower. Capping the candidate at the gap size is what actually guarantees the
move cannot widen the max–min spread; "largest item" and "largest *eligible* item" coincide only when
the biggest item happens to fit under the gap, which is not guaranteed. This was caught in review
against this exact stage, not derived in the abstract — see §7 D0 below.

**Term targeted, and why plausible.** `LoadBalance = 1/(1+CV)`, `CV = stddev/mean` of per-day totals
(`ObjectiveEvaluator.cs:57-74`). Moving minutes from the maximum toward the minimum of that same
distribution is the textbook first-order move to reduce variance around the mean; it directly narrows
the gap between the current max and current min. Side effects (not the stage's target, and not
controlled for): `FatiguePenalty` (`ComputeFatiguePenalty`, `:126-152`) depends on which days end up
*above* the mean and whether they're calendar-adjacent — moving load off the single busiest day can
break a heavy/heavy adjacent pair (improves it) or, if `D_min` is adjacent to another heavy day,
create a new one (worsens it). `ContextContinuity` is affected only if `X`'s subject isn't already
present on `D_min` (`ComputeContextContinuity`, `:84-91`). These are reported, not designed for —
consistent with G2-1: the checkpoint after this stage is evaluated on the full `Score`, not just the
term the stage nominally targets.

**Non-combinatorial, why.** Every selection is a rank-and-take-first (or rank-and-take-first-eligible)
over a list already computable in one pass — `O(days log days)` to rank days, `O(items-on-D_max log)`
to rank items on one day, then a single linear scan for the first item passing the
`SoPhut ≤ maxTotal − minTotal` predicate. The predicate is checked against `maxTotal`/`minTotal`, both
fixed *before* scanning items — it is a filter on a precomputed threshold, not a per-candidate
simulation of the move's outcome. Nothing here evaluates a candidate move's *resulting* score and
compares it against another candidate move's resulting score — that is the shape G2-1 forbids ("nothing
is generated in order to be evaluated"). The rule always looks at **current-state rankings and one
current-state threshold**, computed once, before any move is made.

**Feasibility.** With no deadline preference in the destination choice (per the correction above),
moving `X` to `D_min` can land on either side of `X.HanChot` — this stage makes no attempt to bias
toward the deadline-safe direction and relies entirely on G2-2's admissibility gate (`IsAdmissible`,
unchanged, not redesigned here) for protection. That gate is sufficient regardless: if the move worsens
feasibility, the resulting checkpoint is simply inadmissible and cannot be selected, exactly as G2-2
already guarantees for any stage. This is a deliberate simplification, not an oversight — see the
correction note above for why a deadline-preferring version breaks the stage's actual guarantee (the
non-widening spread proof, §4), which this note weighs as the more important property to keep provable.

**Blast radius.** One new file, `Services/Soe/LoadRebalanceStage.cs`. Reads only `Date`, `SoPhut`, and
`MaTask` — all already on `ScheduledItem`. (`HanChot` is not read: the deadline-preferring destination
step that would have needed it was dropped per the correction above.) No extension to `ScheduledItem`,
no new context parameter on the (still-undesigned) `Optimize` signature.

### Candidate 2 — `N = 2`: `FragmentationCoalesceStage` → `LoadRebalanceStage` (**conditionally recommended**, gated — see §4)

Stage 2 is Candidate 1's `LoadRebalanceStage`, unchanged. Stage 1 is new:

**The move.** Group items by `MaTask`. For each group with ≥2 items, sort its items by `Date`. Scan
consecutive pairs; the first pair found (deterministic left-to-right scan, first match wins — not
"best match") with `Date[i+1] == Date[i].AddDays(1)` is coalesced: the **later** item's minutes are
added to the **earlier** item (same `MaTask`, `Date` = the earlier one, `SoPhut` = sum), and the later
item is removed. At most one merge per pass (the stage returns after the first successful merge across
the whole schedule, scanning tasks in a fixed order — e.g. by `MaTask` GUID — so ties are deterministic).
If no adjacent-day pair exists anywhere, the stage is a no-op.

**Why merge into the earlier day, specifically.** This is a deliberate, deadline-safe direction, not
an arbitrary pick: the later item's `Date` can only move backward or stay put relative to its own
`HanChot`, so a chunk that was on time stays on time, and a chunk that was already overdue becomes
*less* overdue or on-time. Merging into the later day instead would risk moving an on-time chunk past
its deadline for no feasibility benefit — there's no reason to take that risk when the safe direction
costs nothing extra to implement.

**Term targeted, and why plausible.** `FragmentationPenalty = -(Σ(count_per_task − 1)) / totalChunks`
(`ComputeFragmentationPenalty`, `:161-169`). Removing one item from a `MaTask` group with count > 1
directly reduces that group's `count − 1` by one and reduces `totalChunks` by one — both changes move
the ratio toward zero (less negative), i.e. strictly improves this term whenever a merge is available
and `totalChunks` doesn't collapse to zero (the empty-schedule case is separately vacuous at `1.0`/`0.0`
per the evaluator's own doc). Side effect: `SessionQuality` (`ComputeSessionQuality`, `:100-115`)
moves toward the ideal `[25,90]` band if the merged sum lands inside it, or further from it (or past
the `≥180` cutoff to zero) if the merge overshoots — genuinely two-directional, reported not designed
for, same as Candidate 1's side effects.

**Non-combinatorial, why.** The scan is `O(n)` over pre-sorted per-task groups; "first adjacent pair in
a fixed enumeration order" is a deterministic rule over the *input*, not a search over merge outcomes.
The tempting alternative — "merge whichever pair most improves `FragmentationPenalty` or `Total`" —
would require scoring every candidate pair, which is exactly the combinatorial shape G2-1 rules out.
This design deliberately does not do that.

**Feasibility.** Can only improve or hold constant the merged chunk's overdue status (see the
"merge into earlier day" argument above) — this stage is, by construction, no more likely to worsen
`OverdueMinutes` than `C₀` was for the pair it touches. G2-2's admissibility gate remains the backstop
for any part of the schedule this stage doesn't touch.

**Blast radius.** One new file, `Services/Soe/FragmentationCoalesceStage.cs`. Same field budget as
Candidate 1 — no extension needed.

**Structural finding, proved against the allocator, not assumed: the merge target is always at the
allocator's own per-day ceiling before the merge.** Re-reading `GenerateScheduleWithIdentity`'s
placement loop (`WorkloadServiceImpl.cs:161-243`) line by line:

```csharp
int spaceLeft = capacityMinutes - targetDay.TotalMinutes;
int chunk = Math.Min(remainingMinutes, spaceLeft);
...
targetDay.TotalMinutes += chunk;
remainingMinutes -= chunk;
```

and the day-selection filter that gates re-entry to an existing day is `d.TotalMinutes < capacityMinutes`.
Two consequences, both allocator invariants, not corpus observations:

1. **No day's `TotalMinutes` ever exceeds `capacityMinutes`.** `chunk` is capped by `spaceLeft`, so
   `TotalMinutes` after `+= chunk` is always `≤ capacityMinutes`, for every day, always.
2. **Any chunk that is *not* a task's final chunk fills its day to exactly `capacityMinutes`.** The
   `while` loop only continues to place a later chunk of the same task when `remainingMinutes > spaceLeft`
   held at the previous iteration — which means `chunk == spaceLeft` and
   `targetDay.TotalMinutes` lands at exactly `capacityMinutes` before the next iteration picks a new
   day (the `< capacityMinutes` filter now excludes it).

The **earlier** item of any adjacent same-`MaTask` pair `FragmentationCoalesceStage` can find is, by
definition, not that task's final chunk (there's a later one). By (2), its day `D1` is at exactly
`capacityMinutes` before the merge. By (1), `capacityMinutes` is the schedule-wide ceiling **every**
day is bounded by. Moving the later item's `SoPhut` (call it `b > 0`) into `D1` sets
`D1.TotalMinutes = capacityMinutes + b`, which is **strictly greater than every other day in the
schedule** — not just greater than it was, but a value the allocator itself could never have produced.

**What is proved, and what is only strongly expected — kept distinct on purpose.** Proved: the merge
always creates a new schedule-wide maximum, one the allocator could never have produced on its own.
Not proved, and this note stops short of claiming it: that this always strictly worsens
`ObjectiveEvaluator`'s `LoadBalance = 1/(1+CV)` number. `CV` is computed over **used days only**
(`ComputeLoadBalance`, `ObjectiveEvaluator.cs:57-74`) — if `D2` (the later day) loses its only chunk in
the merge, it drops out of that grouping entirely, changing both `n` and the mean, not just one point's
distance from a fixed mean. A simple "pushed the max further from an unchanged mean" argument does not
go through once the used-day *count* can change. The new-strict-maximum result is unconditional; the
`LoadBalance` consequence is strongly expected (a new out-of-allocator-range maximum is the kind of
change `CV` is built to penalize) but not derived to the same certainty, and this note declines to
round that up to "proof" for a term whose actual movement is exactly the kind of thing `OQ1` gates on
data for. This is the direct analogue of `ConstraintValidator.cs`'s own "CHÚ Ý — lỗi lập luận đã sửa"
pattern: an earlier draft of this section argued "merge into the earlier day" was justified purely by
deadline safety and left the load-balance interaction as an unexamined side effect. It is not
unexamined any more, but the examined version is "strongly expected," not "proved," and the note now
says which.

**Why this does not disqualify the stage under G2, and why the stage order in §4 is not cosmetic.**
G2-1's entire premise is that a stage may transiently worsen the schedule so a later stage in the same
pass can recover past it — "every stage runs regardless, so a dip is never rolled back" (G2-3). A
strongly-expected dip on one term (`LoadBalance`, per the proved/expected distinction just above),
traded for a proved gain on another (`FragmentationPenalty`), is exactly the shape G2 was designed to
arbitrate, not a defect to design away. What *is* a real risk: `LoadRebalanceStage` (stage 2), run
immediately after, ranks items on the
new busiest day (now `D1`) by `SoPhut` — and the just-merged item, now sized `a + b`, is a strong
candidate to be exactly what gets picked and moved right back off `D1`, toward a low-loaded day that
may well be `D1`'s own former partner. That would make the pass net-unproductive on precisely the case
it was meant to help (not harmful — G2-2's comparator would simply not select a worse `C2`, and if `C1`
and `C0` also don't win, `k* = 0` — but unproductive). Stage 2's eligibility filter (`SoPhut ≤ maxTotal −
minTotal`, added in Candidate 1 above after the same review round) mitigates this without eliminating
it: if the merged item's size exceeds the new max–min gap — likely, since it just absorbed another
day's full contribution — it is *ineligible* for stage 2 to move, so stage 2 either no-ops or moves a
different, smaller item, leaving the coalesce gain intact. Whether this mitigation is enough to make
the pass net-positive **depends on the corpus's actual chunk sizes and on `w1`/`w5` — which G3 has not
set** — and is not resolvable in this note. See §6 OQ1.

### Candidate 3 — `N = 4`: adds `SessionLengthRepairStage` and `ContextContinuitySwapStage` (**not recommended yet — flagged**)

Same first two stages as Candidate 2, plus:

**`SessionLengthRepairStage` (illustrative sketch, not fully specified).** Targets `SessionQuality`
directly: find the single chunk with `SoPhut` furthest outside `[25,90]` (rank by distance from the
band, deterministic tie-break by `MaTask`); if `< 25`, attempt the same adjacent-day merge
`FragmentationCoalesceStage` already does, scoped to this one chunk; if `≥ 180`, split it in half onto
two days, which requires picking a **second day** — reusing `LoadRebalanceStage`'s "least-loaded used
day" rule keeps this non-combinatorial, but it means this stage is not independent of Stage 2's
selection logic, and a split also directly worsens `FragmentationPenalty` by design (adds a chunk) —
a term Stage 1 is simultaneously trying to improve. This overlap is real, not hypothetical, and is why
this stage is flagged rather than adopted outright.

**`ContextContinuitySwapStage` (flagged, higher risk, sketch only).** Targets `ContextContinuity`
(`1/distinct-subjects-per-day`, averaged). The natural move is "swap the minority-subject item on the
worst-continuity day with an item of that subject on another day" — but *finding* the right swap
partner among possibly many candidate days sharing that subject, if implemented as "pick whichever
swap most improves continuity," is an `O(n²)` search over swap pairs scored by their outcome — precisely
the shape G2-1 forbids. A compliant version exists in principle (e.g., restrict to the single
lowest-`Date` day already containing that subject, found via a one-pass index, no scoring of
alternatives) but it has not been fully worked out here, and its interaction with the day-moving
stages above compounds the ordering-interference problem named in Candidate 2. **This is the one place
in this note where "a stage that's actually implementable and tied to an `ObjectiveScore` term" was not
found to a level of confidence this note is willing to recommend shipping.** If pursued, it needs its
own design pass, not inclusion by default because it "completes the set of five terms."

**Blast radius (both new stages).** Same field budget, no `ScheduledItem` extension — but real design
risk (stage independence, term overlap) that Candidate 1/2 do not carry.

## 3. Design choices made across all candidates, stated once

- **D1 — Whole-item moves only, never split, in `LoadRebalanceStage`.** Splitting would let the stage
  hit `LoadBalance` more precisely, but it manufactures a new chunk — directly fighting
  `FragmentationPenalty`, a term this stage isn't targeting and shouldn't be allowed to quietly worsen
  as an implementation convenience. Simplicity and non-interference with the other candidate's stage
  won over precision here.
- **D2 — No stage ever creates a new day.** Only the allocator opens new days (T3.3's overflow path).
  A quality stage inventing new schedule structure would blur the allocator/optimizer boundary G2-2's
  scope paragraph already drew a hard line around (§"Scope of that claim" in the G2 note).
- **D3 — Deadline-preferring destination/direction choices, only where they're free.**
  `FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s "merge into the earlier day" rule is deadline-safe at no extra cost
  (§2) and is kept. `LoadRebalanceStage`'s destination was originally going to prefer
  `Date ≤ X.HanChot` the same way, but that preference breaks the stage's non-widening-spread proof
  (§2's correction note, §4) — so `LoadRebalanceStage` drops it and always targets `D_min`
  unconditionally, relying entirely on G2-2's admissibility gate for feasibility protection. Neither
  preference is required by G2-2 — the admissibility gate is sufficient regardless — so this is
  "take the free ones, skip the ones that cost something," not a blanket policy.

## 4. Recommendation: **Candidate 1** (`N = 1`, `LoadRebalanceStage`) unconditionally; **Candidate 2 conditionally**, gated on §6 OQ1

This recommendation changed during review of this note (see §7's corrected-reasoning entry) — the
first draft recommended Candidate 2 unconditionally. §2's finding (`FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s
merge target is provably always at the allocator's own per-day ceiling, so the merge provably creates a
new schedule-wide maximum, and — strongly expected but not proved to the same certainty — this is
likely to worsen `LoadBalance`) means Candidate 2's net effect on `Total` depends on `w1` vs `w5`
— weights G3 has not set — and on whether stage 2's eligibility filter actually recovers the dip on
real corpus data. Neither is decidable from this note alone. Recommending it unconditionally would
mean ratifying a stage list whose sign on the epic's own success metric (#8, objective delta vs.
baseline) this note cannot argue is positive.

**Why Candidate 1, unconditionally.** After the eligibility-filter fix (§2), `LoadRebalanceStage` is
provably **non-regressive on its own target term in isolation**: it only ever moves an item sized
`≤ maxTotal − minTotal`, which by construction cannot push the destination past the source's prior
value or the source below the destination's prior value — the max–min spread cannot widen. That is a
closed-form guarantee this note can state without needing G3's weights or corpus data, which is exactly
the bar a DoR-closing recommendation should clear on its own. `N = 1` is also explicitly sanctioned by
the note that created this task ("even if it turns out to be a single stage") — this is that case,
reached on the evidence, not assumed going in.

**What `N = 1` costs, honestly.** With `N = 1`, G2-1's prefix-selection machinery
(`C₀…C_N`, "the best admissible checkpoint of the pass trajectory") only ever has `C₀` and `C₁` to
choose between within a single pass — the machinery built to recover value from a mid-pass dip is
exercised only across passes (via `MaxPasses`), not within one. That is a real under-use of what G2
was designed to justify, and `FragmentationPenalty` is left completely unaddressed. The first draft of
this note treated that as "no cost reason" to prefer `N=1`'s safety — that was wrong; §2's finding is
the cost, and it is real, just not yet known to be *net negative*.

**Three specific consequences, spelled out.** The paragraph above names the under-use in general
terms; these are the three concrete places it shows up, written down so a future stage-2 author does
not have to rediscover them.

1. **At `N = 1`, "run-all, commit-best-prefix" *is* per-step accept/reject** — operationally
   identical to the mechanism G2 §2 explicitly rejected, because the candidate set is exactly
   `{C₀, C₁}`. Both halves of that sentence matter: nothing is wrong (at `N = 1` every candidate
   mechanism coincides, and G2-1 was chosen for what it does at `N > 1`), *and* the implementation is
   currently indistinguishable from the alternative it was chosen over.
2. **`Superseded_ByLaterCheckpoint` (G2-6) is structurally unreachable.** With one stage per pass
   there is no later checkpoint *within* a pass to supersede an earlier one. The reason code is
   implemented and tested for shape, but no corpus run can emit it today.
3. **T3.4's branching-factor assertion reduces to `states == passes × 2`.** `states == passes × N + 1`
   at `N = 1` is close to tautological in its present form: it pins the pass bookkeeping, not the
   branching behaviour it is named for. Read it as a pass-count check, not as evidence that variable
   branching works.

**What this means for whoever adopts Candidate 2.** G2-1's distinguishing guarantees —
all-or-nothing-veto avoidance and superseded-gain retention — have **never run in the mode that
justifies them**. A green branching-factor test at `N = 1` is not evidence that they do. The first
`N = 2` change is the moment those guarantees get their first real exercise, and should be reviewed
as that rather than as an incremental stage addition.

**Candidate 2 as the conditional upgrade.** `FragmentationCoalesceStage` → `LoadRebalanceStage` remains
fully specified (§2) and is the natural `N = 2` upgrade once either (a) G3 ratifies `w1…w5` and the
resulting `w1·(LoadBalance dip)` vs `w5·(FragmentationPenalty gain)` arithmetic can be checked on the
corpus, or (b) corpus telemetry (post-Candidate-1-implementation, using the "observed pass-count
distribution" G2-4 already asks for) shows `FragmentationPenalty` is a material, unaddressed gap. This
is not a deferred *design* question — the stage is specified — it is a deferred *ratification*
question, gated on data this note does not have.

**Order, if/when Candidate 2 is adopted: `FragmentationCoalesceStage` first.** Coalescing first means
`LoadRebalanceStage` (stage 2) runs on the post-merge day totals, giving it the chance to absorb
exactly the dip stage 1 introduced, in the same pass, before anything is committed. This is not merely
a good ordering habit — §2's finding shows the dip is not evenly distributed across passes but
concentrated entirely in `C1`, which strengthens (not weakens) the case for running `LoadRebalanceStage`
second rather than first: reversing the order would let a fragmentation merge run *after* rebalancing
and directly reopen the imbalance rebalancing just fixed, with no stage left in that pass to recover it.

**Why not Candidate 3.** Unchanged from the first draft: `SessionLengthRepairStage` is not independent
of `LoadRebalanceStage`'s day-choice logic and actively works against `FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s
term when it splits; `ContextContinuitySwapStage`'s non-search version is a sketch, not a specification.
Shipping either as part of a ratified DoR would mean ratifying something this note doesn't have
confidence in — worse now, given how much scrutiny the *simpler* Candidate 2 needed to get right.

## 6. Open questions for the owner

1. **(Promoted — this is the ruling Candidate 2's ratification actually turns on.) Given §2's finding
   that `FragmentationCoalesceStage` provably creates a new schedule-wide maximum whenever it fires,
   and is strongly expected (though not proved to the same certainty) to worsen `LoadBalance`'s spread,
   is trading that expected dip for a proved `FragmentationPenalty` gain acceptable to adopt *before*
   G3 sets `w1…w5`, or should Candidate 2 wait for either G3 or corpus telemetry (§4) before
   ratification?** This note recommends waiting (§4) — Candidate 1 ships now, Candidate 2 is fully
   specified but conditionally gated. Two required pieces of evidence before Candidate 2 should be
   revisited, neither gathered here: (a) whether the eligibility filter added to `LoadRebalanceStage`
   in §2 is actually sufficient in practice to avoid a self-defeating oscillation (only checkable once
   both stages exist and run against the corpus); (b) whether adjacent-same-`MaTask`-day pairs even
   arise under the *current* (post-T3.3, earliest-feasible) allocator often enough for
   `FragmentationCoalesceStage` to be non-vacuous — the only committed corpus artifact
   (`docs/reports/data/2026-08-05-soe-t36-baseline.json`) predates T3.3's earliest-feasible placement
   change and is explicitly marked "CHỦ Ý KHÔNG được tái tạo lại" (deliberately frozen as a pre-T3.3
   "before" snapshot), so it cannot answer this for the allocator T3.9 will actually run against. A
   quick corpus mine against the *current* allocator's output (not this artifact) is a cheap,
   worthwhile check before anyone implements Candidate 2 — flagged, not performed, in this docs-only
   pass.
2. **A design-space question this note did not anticipate: does a stage apply its rule once per pass,
   or run its own rule to a local fixpoint before the pass moves to the next stage?** As specified
   (§2), both candidate stages make **at most one move per invocation** — with `N = 1` and
   `MaxPasses = 4`, `Optimize` therefore touches at most 4 items total per call. On a large or
   heavily-imbalanced corpus schedule, that may be too small a delta for success metric #8 to register
   anything, making the seam look inert for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the stage
   design is sound. The alternative — a stage that internally loops its own rule to a fixpoint before
   returning — raises a real question against G2-1's own bright line: "the number of schedule states
   materialized per `Optimize` call is exactly `passes × N + 1`" assumed one state per stage. If a
   stage internally materializes several intermediate lists before returning its one checkpoint-visible
   result, does "materialized" mean *checkpoints* (still `passes × N + 1`, internal iteration invisible
   to the count) or *every intermediate list* (breaks the stated formula, and by extension T3.4's
   branching-factor test)? This note takes the position that "one move per invocation" is the safer
   reading to ship first — it is unambiguously `passes × N + 1` under either interpretation of
   "materialized" — but the fixpoint-per-stage alternative is a real, unexplored design point the owner
   should rule on explicitly rather than have it resolved implicitly by whichever reading an
   implementer happens to pick.
3. **Stage order, if/when Candidate 2 is adopted — `FragmentationCoalesceStage` before
   `LoadRebalanceStage`.** §4 now argues this order is *more* than a habit (it gives the one stage that
   introduces a strongly-expected dip the very next stage's chance to recover it, in the same pass); if
   the owner still prefers the reverse order despite that argument, it doesn't change anything else in
   this note.

## 7. Decisions made (ADR-style)

### D0 — Corrected via review: the first draft's unconditional Candidate-2 recommendation was wrong, and why

- **Why kept in the note instead of silently rewritten:** this repo's own precedent
  (`ConstraintValidator.cs`'s "CHÚ Ý — lỗi lập luận đã sửa") is to leave a corrected argument visible
  rather than erase the wrong one, because the *shape* of the error is instructive on its own. The
  first draft argued Candidate 2 (`FragmentationCoalesceStage` → `LoadRebalanceStage`) unconditionally,
  reasoning that both stages were self-relative (needed no external capacity data) and therefore free
  of hidden assumptions. That reasoning checked the *signature* — neither stage reads a capacity field
  — but not the *data* the allocator actually produces. Advisor review, then independently re-derived
  and proved in §2, found that `FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s merge target is provably always already
  at the allocator's own per-day ceiling — an allocator *invariant*, not a corpus accident — so the
  merge always creates a new schedule-wide maximum. "Self-relative" was the wrong axis to check;
  "what does the input data structurally guarantee" was the one that mattered.
- **What for:** the recommendation now separates what this note can prove without data (§4's Candidate
  1 argument — non-regressive on its own target term, closed-form) from what it can only specify and
  flag pending data (§4's Candidate 2 — fully designed, ratification gated on §6 OQ1). That split is
  the actual deliverable of the correction: not "Candidate 2 is bad" (it isn't — G2-1 is built to
  arbitrate exactly this kind of dip-then-recover trade) but "this note cannot certify Candidate 2's
  net sign, and said so instead of asserting a recommendation it couldn't back."
- **Experience:** the review-catching mechanism here was the same one this repo has used before — an
  advisor pass reading the transcript, not a fresh read of the same reasoning by the same author. The
  fix was cheap once found (a one-line eligibility filter on `LoadRebalanceStage`, §2) but the finding
  itself required tracing the allocator's actual loop invariants, not reasoning about the stage
  abstractly from the `ObjectiveEvaluator` formulas alone — the same "verify code, not documents about
  code" lesson (L1) the G2 note's own D5 names.

**Second correction, same review cycle, own bullet because it's a different failure mode.** The
eligibility-filter fix above was itself checked by a second advisor pass and found incomplete: it was
derived assuming the destination is always the globally least-loaded day, but the draft at that point
still had `LoadRebalanceStage`'s destination *prefer* `Date ≤ X.HanChot` (mirroring
`FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s deadline-safety habit). That preference can route the move to a day far
more loaded than the true minimum, and the concrete counter-example in §2's correction note
(`maxTotal=300, minTotal=60, destTotal=280, X.SoPhut=200` → spread widens from 240 to 420) shows the
eligibility threshold's own proof silently stopped applying once the destination could be anything
other than the day the threshold was computed against. Fixed by dropping the deadline preference from
this one stage (§2, §3 D3) — cheap, and the closed-form guarantee §4 rests on is restored, this time
checked against the actual destination the spec produces, not the one an earlier paragraph assumed.
**Pattern worth naming for future notes in this series:** a fix that closes one gap in a proof is not
automatically compatible with every other design choice already sitting in the same stage — recheck
the whole spec against the tightened proof, not just the line that changed.

### D1 — Recommend `N = 1` unconditionally, `N = 2` conditionally — not the most complete (`N = 4`) option, and not `N = 2` unconditionally

- **Why:** `N = 4` reaches for completeness (touching more of `w1…w5`) at the cost of two stages whose
  non-combinatorial selection rules are sketches, not specifications — ratifying that would mean
  ratifying uncertainty this note itself flags. `N = 2` unconditionally was the first draft's answer
  and is now known (§2, §7 D0) to trade a provable dip for an unproven-sign net gain — not disqualified,
  but not certifiable from this note alone either. `N = 1` is the one candidate this note can recommend
  without appeal to data it doesn't have: its stage is provably non-regressive on its own target term,
  in closed form, today.
- **What for:** whoever implements `Optimize()` next gets an unconditional, fully-specified `N = 1`
  stage list to build now, plus a fully-specified `N = 2` upgrade with an explicit, named precondition
  for when to adopt it — rather than a single number presented as settled when part of its justification
  was actually undetermined.
- **Experience:** the two stages examined in enough depth to be specified precisely (`LoadBalance`,
  `FragmentationPenalty`) are the two whose formulas are one-line and closed-form
  (`ObjectiveEvaluator.cs:57-74` and `:161-169`) — but "closed-form formula" turned out not to imply
  "closed-form, provably-safe *move*." `LoadRebalanceStage` needed one correction (the eligibility
  filter) to earn its non-regressive claim; `FragmentationCoalesceStage` needed the full proof in §2
  before its cost could even be named, let alone weighed. The two terms left out of both candidates
  (`SessionQuality`, `ContextContinuity`) were flagged as harder for a *different*, weaker reason
  (their plausible moves reach for another stage's logic or a swap-partner search) — this note's
  confidence in that earlier, softer flag is now higher, having seen how much scrutiny even the
  "easy" two needed to get right.

### D2 — Every recommended stage stays inside `ScheduledItem`'s existing seven fields

- **Why:** G2's `Optimize(schedule) → (schedule, report)` signature, as ratified, takes a schedule and
  nothing else. A stage that needs external capacity context would either force a signature change G2
  never authorized, or force an extension to `ScheduledItem` that every existing consumer
  (`IConstraintValidator`, `IObjectiveEvaluator`, their tests) would then carry for a field only one
  stage uses. Both are real costs; neither was necessary once the design leaned on the same
  self-relative approach `ObjectiveEvaluator` already uses.
- **What for:** both the recommended Candidate 1 and the conditionally-gated Candidate 2 (§4) have
  **zero** blast radius against the seams Cards D/E/C already shipped — confirmed empirically in §1,
  not just argued. Whoever ratifies this note is ratifying something that cannot regress existing tests
  by construction, only add new ones.
- **Experience:** the temptation, once `LoadRebalanceStage` was sketched, was to reach for "respect the
  original per-day capacity ceiling" as an obviously-good idea — until §1's check confirmed no such
  ceiling is enforced anywhere downstream of the allocator today. Building it in here would have been
  the same "capacity/calendar predicate built ahead of need" pattern Card D explicitly declined —
  worth resisting twice, not just once.

### D3 — Name the merge-direction and destination-preference rules explicitly, don't leave them implicit — and check each one actually costs nothing before keeping it

- **Why:** "merge two adjacent chunks" or "move a chunk to a less-loaded day" sound like one decision
  each, but each hides a direction/destination choice with a feasibility consequence (§2, Candidate 2's
  "merge into the earlier day" argument; §2, Candidate 1's destination correction). Leaving these
  implicit would hand the implementer a choice this note is supposed to have already made — the entire
  reason T3.9 exists is that "an implementer inventing a stage list unreviewed" was one of the two
  failure modes the T3.3-scope-narrowed note named as a risk of *not* writing this note.
- **What for:** the implementer of `Optimize()` has a specification for both stages precise enough that
  "which direction does the merge go" / "which day is the destination" is not a judgment call left for
  code review to catch.
- **Experience:** the two direction/destination choices did **not** turn out to be free in the same
  way. `FragmentationCoalesceStage`'s deadline-safe merge direction cost nothing to add — a genuine free
  win, kept. `LoadRebalanceStage`'s deadline-preferring destination looked like the same kind of free
  win by analogy, and was not: it silently invalidated the stage's own non-widening-spread proof (§2's
  correction, §7's second-correction entry). The lesson isn't "prefer deadline-safety rules," it's
  "each one has to be checked against what the *rest of the stage* is trying to guarantee, individually
  — a pattern that was free once doesn't transfer by resemblance to a different stage with a different
  proof obligation."
